        Why these drills

        Reacting is a chain: notice, decide, move. The links respond to practice differently, so each drill works one of them.

        • Draw is the benchmark. Pure see-it-tap-it speed has the least room to improve, so it gets eight taps and no more. The wait is random so you can't time it.
        • Lights adds a choice among nine places. Decision time is where most of the trainable milliseconds are.
        • Stop light adds holding back. Getting faster only counts if you don't also get trigger-happy.
        • Switch adds a rule that sometimes flips, so you practise deciding under conflict.
        • Intercept hides the ball mid-flight. People who look fast in sport are mostly predicting, and this is practice at predicting.
        • Strobe shows the ball only in short glimpses. It borrows the idea behind strobe-glasses training, the method with the most recent support for reaction time. A flickering screen is an approximation of that, not the tested method.

        How it adapts

        Each hit shortens the time you're allowed by 4%. Each miss lengthens it by 18%. That settles where you succeed about four times in five, which keeps you at the edge of what you can do. Each drill remembers where you finished and starts the next run just below it. A personal best only counts when accuracy is 80% or better.

        What it won't do

        Screen drills reliably make you better at screen drills and at similar tasks. Reviews have not found good evidence that this carries over to performance in a sport. Use it as a sharpener and a self-test alongside real practice.

        Phones add their own touch and display delay, so your times here will run slower than a lab figure. Compare yourself with yourself, on the same device.

        Sources: Dye, Green and Bavelier, Current Directions in Psychological Science, 2009. Bediou et al., Psychological Bulletin, 2018. Hilgard et al., Collabra: Psychology, 2019. Fransen, Sports Medicine, 2024. Stroboscopic training meta-analysis, Frontiers in Psychology, 2025.
